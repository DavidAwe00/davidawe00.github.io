How this works & sources
  • All amounts are in today's dollars; growth uses your after-inflation return. Contributions assume your salary keeps pace with inflation.
  • Social Security is a rough estimate: it treats your current salary (capped at the 2026 taxable maximum, $184,500) as your average indexed earnings and applies SSA's 2026 formula — 90% of the first $1,286/month, 32% up to $7,749, 15% above — then adjusts for claiming age (full retirement age is 67 if born 1960 or later; claiming at 62 cuts benefits 30%, waiting to 70 adds 24%). Get your real estimate at ssa.gov/myaccount.
  • Savings needed = (spending − Social Security) ÷ withdrawal rate. The "4% rule" comes from William Bengen's 1994 research; the success rates here are recomputed from 1928–2025 data (60% S&P 500 / 40% 10-year Treasuries, rebalanced yearly, inflation-adjusted withdrawals, 30-year retirements).
  • Benchmarks: Fidelity's guideline of saving 1× salary by 30, 3× by 40, 6× by 50, 8× by 60, and 10× by 67.

How this works & sources
  • Traditional: you contribute pre-tax, it grows, and withdrawals are taxed at your future rate. Roth: you pay tax now (your current bracket), then growth and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. To compare fairly, both cost the same out of your take-home pay: the Roth contribution is reduced by today's tax.
  • Tax brackets and the standard deduction ($16,100 single / $32,200 joint) are 2026 figures from the IRS (Rev. Proc. 2025-32). Roth IRA contributions phase out at $153,000–$168,000 (single) and $242,000–$252,000 (joint) of modified AGI in 2026 (IRS Notice 2025-67).
  • Simplified: uses your marginal bracket for the whole contribution and ignores state taxes, credits, and required minimum distributions.
