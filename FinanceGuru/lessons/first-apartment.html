Renting Your First Apartment

What you can afford, what's in a lease, and how to get your deposit back.

Start the interactive lesson →Leer en español →

What can you afford?

HUD considers a household cost-burdened when housing takes more than 30% of income. The number of cost-burdened renters is at a record high, so it's worth planning carefully.

Many landlords also want your monthly income to be about 3 times the rent. For $1,000 rent, that's $3,000 a month before taxes.

Count the extras too: electricity, gas, water, internet, renters insurance, and parking can add hundreds a month.

Sources: HUD; Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, America's Rental Housing 2026.

Try it: the First Apartment budget adds up rent, move-in costs, and a roommate split.

The cash you need to move in

Before you get the keys, you may need:

  • Application fees for each place you apply to.
  • First month's rent, and sometimes the last month's too.
  • A security deposit, often about one month's rent.
  • Utility deposits or setup fees, moving costs, and basic furniture.

Moving in can easily cost two to three times the monthly rent, so save for it ahead of time.

Read the lease before you sign

A lease is a contract. Check:

  • Length, and what it costs to break the lease early.
  • Which bills you pay and which the landlord pays.
  • Late fees, guest and pet rules, and who handles repairs.
  • With roommates, everyone on the lease is usually responsible for the full rent. If one roommate stops paying, the others may have to cover it.

Get every promise in writing. Never send a deposit for a place you haven't seen or before you've signed a lease (see Spotting Scams).

Get your deposit back

  • Document move-in: take dated photos and video of every room, and note existing damage on a move-in checklist the landlord signs.
  • Report repairs in writing (email or a text you keep).
  • At move-out, clean, repair what you damaged, and photograph everything again.

Most states set a deadline for returning your deposit and require landlords to list any deductions. Normal wear and tear usually can't be charged. Check your state's rules.

Your rights as a renter

The federal Fair Housing Act makes it illegal to refuse to rent to you, or to set different terms, because of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, disability, or having children. You can report discrimination to HUD.

States and cities add their own protections, such as how much notice a landlord must give before entering or raising rent. Search for "tenant rights" plus your state.

Renters insurance covers your belongings (about $170 a year; see Insurance 101).
