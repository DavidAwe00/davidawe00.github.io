Money & Relationships: Roommates, Partners & Lending

Split costs fairly, lend without losing friends, and know what co-signing really means.

Start the interactive lesson →Leer en español →

Money is awkward. Talk anyway.

About 7 in 10 U.S. adults have lent money or covered a group expense expecting to be paid back. More than half of them had something go wrong: 44% lost money and 26% said it damaged the relationship.

Most of these problems start with an unclear deal. A five-minute conversation up front prevents most of them.

Source: Bankrate Financial Taboos Survey (YouGov, September 2025; published October 27, 2025).

Roommates: agree before you move in

Put a simple roommate agreement in writing before move-in day:

  • How rent is split (a bigger room or private bathroom often pays more).
  • Who pays which bills (electricity, internet, streaming) and by what date.
  • Shared groceries and supplies, guests, and quiet hours.
  • What happens if someone moves out early.

Remember: if you're all on the lease, the landlord can usually collect the full rent from any of you (see Renting Your First Apartment).

Try it: the First Apartment budget splits rent and move-in costs with roommates.

Lending to friends and family

  • Only lend what you could afford to never get back. If you can't, it isn't extra money.
  • Consider giving a smaller amount as a gift instead. No debt, no awkward reminders.
  • If it's a loan, write it down: the amount and when it will be paid back. A text message that both of you reply to counts.
  • Never lend money you need for your own rent or bills.

It's okay to say no: "I can't swing that right now, but I can help you figure out a plan."

Co-signing means it's your debt

When you co-sign a loan, credit card, or lease, you promise to pay if the other person doesn't. The FTC warns you may have to pay the full amount, plus late fees or collection costs, and the debt can show up on your credit report.

By law, lenders must give co-signers a "Notice to Cosigner" for many loans. Its message is blunt: if the borrower doesn't pay, you will have to.

Only co-sign if you could pay the whole thing yourself. And never share your Social Security number or account logins "just this once," even with family.

Source: Federal Trade Commission, "Cosigning a Loan FAQs."

Partners: yours, mine, and ours

Before you combine money with a partner, talk about income, debts, credit, goals, and spending style. Many couples use a mix: a joint account for shared bills plus separate accounts for personal spending.

Keep some savings and credit in your own name. Warning signs of financial control include one person taking all the money, demanding your passwords, or running up debt in your name.

If that's happening to you, the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, thehotline.org) offers free, confidential help.
