Spotting Scams & Protecting Your Identity

The red flags every scam shares, and what to do if one gets you.

Start the interactive lesson →Leer en español →

Scams are big business

In 2025, Americans filed 3 million fraud reports with the Federal Trade Commission and reported losing $15.9 billion, the most ever. The most common type was imposter scams: someone pretending to be your bank, a delivery company, the government, a boss, or a friend.

The top way scammers made first contact was a text message. The biggest losses came from bank payments, followed by cryptocurrency.

Source: FTC testimony to Congress's Joint Economic Committee, March 25, 2026 (Consumer Sentinel 2025 data). Most fraud is never reported, so real losses are higher.

The four red flags

Almost every scam uses some mix of these:

  • Pressure to act now: "Your account will be closed today," "Pay in the next hour or a warrant is issued."
  • A strange way to pay: gift cards, crypto, wire transfers, or payment apps. The FTC says only scammers demand gift cards.
  • A request for codes or passwords: real companies never ask for the one-time code they just texted you.
  • Too good to be true: a job that pays $500/day for "liking posts," a prize you never entered, a cheap apartment you can't see in person.
Practice: sort real-looking messages in the Spot the Scam game.

Scams aimed at young people

  • Fake jobs and "task" gigs: they pay you a little, then ask you to deposit money to "unlock" bigger earnings.
  • Fake checks: someone "overpays" you and asks you to send back the difference. The check bounces days later, and you owe the bank.
  • Online shopping and resale: concert tickets, sneakers, or game items that never arrive, paid with an app that can't reverse it.
  • Rental scams: a "landlord" wants a deposit before you've seen the place or signed a lease.

Payment apps work like handing over cash. If you send money to a scammer, you may not get it back.

Lock down your identity

  • Don't use the link or number in a message. Go to the company's app or the number on your card instead.
  • Turn on two-step login and use a different password for every account (a password manager helps).
  • Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. It's free by federal law, and it stops anyone from opening accounts in your name. You can unfreeze it anytime you apply for credit.
  • Check your accounts weekly, and your free credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com.

If a scam gets you

Act fast, and don't be embarrassed: scammers are professionals.

  1. Call your bank or payment app right away and ask them to stop or reverse the payment.
  2. Change passwords on any account you shared, starting with email.
  3. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If someone used your identity, get a recovery plan at IdentityTheft.gov.

Watch out for "recovery" services that promise to get your money back for a fee. That's often a second scam.
