Inflation: The Invisible Tax

Why a dollar buys less every year — and how to keep up.

Start the interactive lesson →Leer en español →

Prices creep up

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. The U.S. tracks it with the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which follows the cost of a big basket of everyday goods and services.

Overall prices in 2025 were about 3.9 times what they were in 1980. Something that cost $100 then cost roughly $390 in 2025.

Try it: the inflation calculator converts any amount between 1926 and 2025.

Small numbers, big effect

From 1928 to 2025, inflation averaged about 3% a year. That sounds tiny, but it cuts a dollar's buying power in half roughly every 23 years.

Some years are much worse: prices rose 13.3% in 1979 and 6.5% in 2022. And during the early Great Depression, prices actually fell (deflation).

How to beat it

Money that just sits still loses value. After inflation, 1928–2025:

  • Cash in T-bills: about +0.3% a year — barely kept up.
  • 10-year Treasury bonds: about +1.5% a year.
  • Stocks (S&P 500): about +6.8% a year.

That's why long-term money usually belongs in investments, while cash is for emergencies and short-term goals. Higher income growth (raises, skills) is the other big inflation-fighter.
