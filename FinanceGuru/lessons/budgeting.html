Budgeting Basics

Tell your money where to go instead of wondering where it went.

Start the interactive lesson →Leer en español →

What a budget really is

A budget isn't a punishment. It's a plan for your money made before you spend it.

Without a plan, money tends to leak away on small things. With one, you decide what matters most to you — and fund that first.

A plan also gives you a cushion. In 2025, 63% of U.S. adults said they could cover a surprise $400 expense with cash or its equivalent. The rest would have to borrow, sell something, or couldn't pay at all.

Source: Federal Reserve, Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025 (May 2026).

The 50/30/20 rule

A simple starting point for your take-home (after-tax) pay:

  • 50% Needs — rent, groceries, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments.
  • 30% Wants — eating out, streaming, hobbies, travel.
  • 20% Savings & extra debt payments — your future self.

Example: Jordan's first full-time job pays $2,400 a month after taxes. That's $1,200 for needs, $720 for wants, and $480 for savings. If rent alone is $1,100, needs will run over 50%, so Jordan trims wants first, not savings.

The 50/30/20 guideline was popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in All Your Worth (2005).

Try it: the Budget Planner splits your income for you, and the First Paycheck Plan builds one from your real take-home pay.

Pick a method that fits you

50/30/20 is one way to budget. Two other popular methods:

  • Zero-based budget: give every dollar a job (bills, food, fun, savings) until income minus your plan equals $0. More work, but nothing goes unplanned.
  • Envelope budget: put a set amount for each category in its own envelope (cash, or separate accounts or "buckets" in a banking app). When an envelope is empty, that category is done for the month.

The best method is the one you'll actually keep using. You can switch any time.

Practice: fill the envelopes in the Envelope Budget game.

Pay yourself first

The most powerful budgeting habit: move money to savings the day you get paid, automatically. Then spend what's left.

If you wait to save "whatever is left over," there usually isn't anything left over.

Track where it really goes

A budget is a guess until you check it. Once a month, open your bank or payment app and sort last month's spending into needs, wants, and savings.

  • Look for small repeat purchases: $6 a day on snacks or coffee is about $180 a month.
  • List every subscription. Free trials that turned into charges are easy to miss.
  • Adjust next month's plan to match real life. Changing a budget isn't failing; it's how budgets work.
Try it: add up your subscriptions in the Subscription Audit.

When your pay changes every month

Tips, gig work, and seasonal jobs don't pay the same amount every time. To budget anyway:

  1. Plan around a low month, not your best one. Cover needs with what you can count on.
  2. In good months, put the extra into a buffer savings account.
  3. In slow months, pay yourself from the buffer so your spending stays steady.

Gig and freelance pay usually has no taxes taken out, so set some aside for taxes too (see Side Hustles).
