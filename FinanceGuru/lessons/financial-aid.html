Paying for College: FAFSA, Grants & Loans

Get the free money first, read your aid offer, and borrow only what you need.

Start the interactive lesson →Leer en español →

Free money first

Pay for school in this order:

  1. Grants and scholarships: money you don't pay back.
  2. Work-study: a part-time job, often on campus, that you earn as you work.
  3. Federal student loans: borrowed money with fixed rates and protections.
  4. Private loans: last, and only if you still have a gap.

Every step down this list costs you more, so use up each one before moving to the next.

The FAFSA unlocks it

The FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the form for federal grants, work-study, and federal loans, and many states and colleges use it for their own aid. It's free at StudentAid.gov. Never pay a website to file it.

The 2027–28 FAFSA opened on September 23, 2026 and covers school from July 1, 2027, to June 30, 2028. It uses your family's 2025 tax information. The federal deadline is June 30, 2028, but some state and college aid runs out much sooner, so file as early as you can.

You'll file a new FAFSA for every year of school.

Source: U.S. Department of Education (press release, Sept. 23, 2026) and StudentAid.gov.

Grants and scholarships

A Pell Grant is federal aid for students with financial need. For 2026–27 the most you can get is $7,395 a year, and you don't pay it back.

Your state and your college may add their own grants. Scholarships come from colleges, employers, community groups, and nonprofits. Apply to several: small ones add up and often have fewer applicants.

Scholarship scams ask you to pay a fee to apply or "guarantee" an award. Real scholarships don't charge you (see Spotting Scams).

Source: U.S. Department of Education, Dear Colleague Letter GEN-26-01 (Jan. 30, 2026).

Read your aid offer

Each college you're admitted to sends an aid offer. Offers mix grants, work-study, and loans together, so do the math yourself:

Net price = cost of attendance − grants and scholarships

Cost of attendance includes tuition, fees, housing, food, books, and transportation. Loans are not a discount: they cover part of the net price now, and you repay them with interest later.

Compare colleges by net price, not by the sticker price or the total "aid" amount. If your family's situation has changed, you can ask the financial aid office to take another look.

Run the numbers: see what a degree pays back in the College ROI calculator.

Federal loans before private loans

  • Subsidized loans are for students with financial need. The government pays the interest while you're in school at least half-time.
  • Unsubsidized loans don't depend on need, but interest builds from the day the money is paid out.

For 2026–27, both carry a fixed 6.52% rate for undergraduates. A dependent first-year student can borrow up to $5,500 in federal loans, of which up to $3,500 can be subsidized.

Private loans often need a cosigner, may have variable rates, and lack federal repayment options. Borrow only what you need, even if you're offered more.

Source: U.S. Department of Education, interest rates for Direct Loans first disbursed July 1, 2026 – June 30, 2027; StudentAid.gov loan limits.
